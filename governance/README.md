# How Rowih changes

Rowih changes in two tiers, because its guarantees are properties of the whole code space and not of any one word. The core changes only by release, through a proposal that meets the rule of change and passes the gate; the periphery changes wherever agents use the language, and asks nothing of this repository. This document states what each tier holds, who may propose a change and who merges it, the rule a change must meet, the gate it must pass, and how dialects and new words arise. The [specification](../specification/README.md) states the language as released; this document states how the specification comes to change.

## The two tiers

The **core** is the grammar, with its classes, members, productions and structural words; the protected words and the check words; the shared lexicon; the protocol; the phonology and the shapes of words; and the writing system. A change to the core can break a property on which every message depends, and none of these properties is visible in the word that breaks it. Every word stands at a distance of at least two symbols from every other, and every protected word at three, so that one wrong symbol never turns a word into another ([The sounds and the shapes of words](../specification/sounds-and-words.md#the-distances-between-words)). Every word's length is fixed by its opening, so that a reader finds where each word ends without a separator. The number of critical forms, which grows with each release, must stay below the move check word's modulus less one, and the codes from which forms are drawn leave a fixed room for new words ([The lexicon](../specification/lexicon.md#the-codes-from-which-forms-are-drawn); [Messages, protocol and transport](../specification/protocol.md#the-move-check-word)). A word added carelessly can therefore cost a guarantee in a message that never uses it, and the core changes only under the rule and the gate below.

The **periphery** is the dialects of communities, the words agents define within an exchange, the compounds and derivations they coin from the productive patterns, and the conventional ways they find of saying things. It changes wherever agents use the language, and a hearer reads it from the core: a coined word from its parts, a defined word from its definition, and a dialect's words from a release the message names under its digest. A dialect enters this repository only to be numbered and published, and its releases pass the same gate as the core's ([Dialects](#dialects)).

## Who proposes and who merges

A change to the core, and a release of a dialect, is proposed by an agent. The repository states the rule, and a proposal names the agent that wrote it and the operator that runs it. A host can enforce the form of a proposal but not who wrote it, so the rule is a policy the gate supports and cannot prove. Anyone may report a defect of the toolchain or a weakness in a guarantee, and a community may ask for a dialect number.

The maintainers of the repository merge a proposal that meets its rule of change and passes the gate, and nothing that does not. That is a custody and not an editorship: they check that each requirement is met, that every objection of the review has a written answer, and that the gate passed over the proposal's final commit, and they do not choose among designs that meet the rule.

## A proposal

A proposal is a file in [`proposals/`](../proposals/README.md), in the format [the format of a proposal](proposals.md) fixes, added by the pull request that makes the change. It states what changes, the communicative problem it answers, the rule of change it meets and the data it regenerates, and it carries the independent reviewer's reading where its kind needs one. One proposal makes one change, and the file stays in the repository as the record of the change once it is merged. `tools/check_proposal.py` checks the format.

A change that makes no release of any part of the language needs no proposal: a fix of the implementations, the tools or the tests, or a change to a document's wording that alters no rule. It comes as a pull request with the tests that show what it fixes, passes the gate like any other, and states the packages' new version in its entry in `CHANGELOG.md`.

## The rule of change

A change within the architecture adds to the language without altering what it guarantees: a root, an idiom, an act word, a member appended to a class, a feature of the protocol, a release of a dialect. A change is **architectural** when it alters a guarantee or an invariant the specification states ([Conformance](../specification/conformance.md#the-guarantees)), the meaning of a released form, or a rule the specification fixes for a major version, such as the codes, the order of the critical forms or the computation of a check word. Every change meets the first, second, fourth and fifth requirements below; a change to the core's form or grammar also meets the third; and an architectural change meets all five in full.

1. **A reason that is evidence.** The proposal shows a communicative problem, what agents need to say that the release in force says only badly or not at all, with messages that show it, a measurement, a failed conformance run or a demonstrated contradiction. That a rule is hard to implement is never by itself a reason, since the language's guarantees rest on the rules that are hardest to keep.
2. **A check of what it touches.** Every guarantee and invariant the specification states that names what changes ([Conformance](../specification/conformance.md#the-invariants)) is checked, and the proposal says how each holds after the change, or, for an architectural change, how it changes and what replaces it.
3. **An independent review.** A change to the core's form or grammar is read by an independent reviewer: an agent that did not write the proposal, given only the grammar summary and a sample of the corpus, which `tools/review_linguisticity.py` writes, and the four questions "Does the language actually behave like a language?", "Is it merely a programming language?", "Is it merely a cipher?" and "Is it merely an English relexification?", each to be answered yes, no or in part with its evidence, without being told the answers the design intends. An answer of no to the first question, or of yes to any other, is a departure, and blocks the change until it is remedied. Each ground of an answer of in part, and each objection the reviewer raises, is answered in writing in the proposal, by a change or by a reason. An architectural change is also read by a reviewer who is given the proposal and the specification it changes, and whose objections are answered in the same way.
4. **A new version.** The change makes a new release of each part it changes, under the rule of compatibility ([Versions and releases](#versions-and-releases)), and an entry in `CHANGELOG.md` states the release's version and date, the parts it affects, the change, the reason, the compatibility impact and the migration it requires.
5. **The text it replaces, kept.** The specification is rewritten for the new release, and the text the change replaces stays at the tag of the release it belonged to and in the repository's history.

## The regenerated data

The data in `language/`, `lexicon/` and `corpus/` are built from the sources in `tools/`, and are never edited by hand. A proposal edits the sources and commits what the rebuild writes: `tools/build_language.py`, `tools/build_domains.py`, `tools/build_corpus.py`, `tools/compute_maxima.py`, `tools/build_negative.py`, `tools/gloss_corpus.py`, `tools/measure_usage.py` and `tools/count_classes.py`, in that order. `tools/check_rebuild.py` rebuilds a copy of the repository in that order and refuses data that differ from what the sources build. The tables of the specification are rewritten by `tools/build_specification.py` and the skill's references by `tools/build_skill.py`, and the tests refuse either when it is stale.

Each release appends its additions after every earlier word, so that no released form moves: the build assigns each new form from the code it belongs to, at the distances the specification requires from every word before it, and the list of critical forms, the act words among them, only grows at its end, so that no released word's code changes. A release's additions are written in a source of their own in `tools/`, which the first proposal of the release creates and its build step reads after every earlier release's.

## The gate

The gate is every run a release's acceptance makes, over the code and data of the commit it tags. Continuous integration runs the gate on every pull request and on every commit to the public repository's `main`, and a proposal is merged only when every run its kind requires passes over its final commit.

| Run | What it shows | Command | Required of |
|---|---|---|---|
| The suite | Every test of both implementations passes, the release checks of the lexicon among them, and the specification's tables and the skill's references are current | `uv run pytest`; in `typescript/`, `npm test` and `npx tsc --noEmit -p .` | every proposal |
| The rebuild | The data are what the sources build | `python3 tools/check_rebuild.py` | every proposal |
| The dependencies | No package of either lock file has a known advisory | `uv audit --frozen`; in `typescript/`, `npm audit` | every proposal |
| The format | The proposal is in the fixed format, with its review where its kind needs one | `python3 tools/check_proposal.py --merge proposals/NNNN-*.md` | every proposal |
| The two implementations | The Python and TypeScript implementations write the same record of every corpus message, every negative item and a seeded sample of their edits ([Conformance](../specification/conformance.md#the-two-implementations)) | `python3 tools/compare_implementations.py` | every proposal |
| The planted defects | Every defect planted in the reference implementation is caught by the tests that name it ([Conformance](../specification/conformance.md#the-planted-defects)) | `python3 tools/run_mutants.py` | every proposal |
| The tokenizer panel | Every pinned tokenizer returns every message of the conformance sets byte for byte, and the control returns none | `python3 tools/tokenizer_panel.py check` | every proposal |
| The single-edit enumeration | No single edit of a symbol or a word changes a critical value silently where the integrity check value is verified ([Conformance](../specification/conformance.md#the-enumerations)) | `python3 tools/run_enumerations.py both` | a change of grammar, lexicon, protocol, phonology or a dialect's compressed forms |
| The originality of words | No new word of one morpheme is close to a frequent word of a major language or a word of a constructed language with a related meaning | `python3 tools/compare_roots.py compare` | a change that adds roots |
| The originality of glyphs | No glyph is near-identical to a letter of an encoded writing system | `python3 tools/compare_glyphs.py` | a change of the writing system |
| The reviewer's reading | The four questions are answered without a departure, and every ground and objection has its answer | `python3 tools/review_linguisticity.py DIRECTORY`, then the reading recorded with `record` | a change to the core's form or grammar |

The two implementations' comparison and the enumeration are the long runs, of hours and not minutes, and they run over the proposal's final commit like the rest. Continuous integration divides each among jobs with `--shard K/N`: a job checks the K-th of N interleaved parts of the run and fails on any fault it finds, so that the parts together are the whole run. A release runs the gate again over the commit it tags, and the reports in `corpus/reports/` stand for what is tagged. A run may be carried over from the gate of the release before only where the release changes neither the code the run executes, compared without its comments, nor any data or message the run reads: every run reports the same results from the same code and data, its timings apart, so it would write the same report. The release's entry in `CHANGELOG.md` names each report carried over and the comparison that allows it, and every other run is made again over the tagged commit.

## Versions and releases

The language, the lexicon, the protocol, the phonology, the writing system and each dialect carry separate versions, each a major and a minor number. Within a major version every expression that was valid stays valid with the same meaning: a form may be deprecated, which the lexicon marks and a reader reports, but a deprecated form stays interpretable. A change that makes a valid expression invalid, or changes its meaning, raises the major number and states the migration it requires; every other change raises the minor number. Every construction added to the language carries a flag that says whether a reader may pass over it or must understand it, so that an older reader knows what it can skip ([Messages, protocol and transport](../specification/protocol.md#versions-and-features)). Every message states the releases it is written in, and a release declares the earlier releases it reads, each of which it must read exactly as that release recorded its own corpus: `tools/record_release.py` writes the record when a release is made, and the tests hold every later release to it.

A release is a commit that the maintainers tag after the gate has passed over it, each run made again or carried over as [the gate](#the-gate) allows, and a tag never moves. The packages `rowih` take versions of their own under semantic versioning, and state the releases of the language they implement.

## Dialects

A dialect is a community's numbered and versioned release of technical terms and, where it gives them, compressed forms ([Dialects](../specification/dialects.md)). The registry of dialect numbers is [`governance/dialects.json`](dialects.json). A number names one community and is never reused or reassigned; the next number goes to the next community that asks, in order, and may be assigned before the dialect's first release. A community asks by stating its name and what its dialect is for, and the maintainers add its entry to the registry. The number keeps a dialect one community's, and the digest of each release, not its number, keeps a message from being read under another community's release.

A release of a dialect is proposed like a change to the core, with the kind *dialect*. Its terms are listed in the sources by the roots and suffixes that form them, and the build forms each term, assigns its compressed form where the dialect gives them, and computes the release's digest: forms are never written by hand, since each must stand at the specification's distances from every word of every release. A release repeats every term and form of the one before it and appends its own, so that earlier releases and their digests stay valid, and it deprecates rather than removes. It must meet the requirements the specification states ([Dialects](../specification/dialects.md#what-a-dialect-release-may-hold)), which the tests check for every release in the repository. A dialect release changes no word of the core and needs no reviewer's reading, and it is compatible by construction.

## Slang and new words

The language already has three kinds of informal speech: the casual register, the compressed forms of the conversational dialect, and the idioms, whose constructed histories make them conventional ([The lexicon](../specification/lexicon.md#registers)).

A new word arises by three routes, as words enter a natural language. An agent may coin a compound or a derivation from the productive patterns at any time, and any hearer reads it from its parts ([The lexicon](../specification/lexicon.md#word-formation)). An agent may define a term by the act of definition, scoped to the exchange or to a dialect, which gives the word a meaning for those who hold the definition ([The lexicon](../specification/lexicon.md#coining-a-word-by-definition)). And a word that spreads may be proposed for a dialect's next release or for the shared lexicon, through the rule of change and the gate. Coinage is free, spread is in a community's hands, and standardisation is governed.
